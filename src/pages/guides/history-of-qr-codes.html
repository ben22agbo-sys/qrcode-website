The origin of the QR code.

The squares on every café table started life in a Japanese car parts factory. Here's how a team at Denso designed them, and why the design still works so well.

Published 20 November 2026

A problem with barcodes

In the early 1990s, Denso, a Japanese maker of car parts, was tracking parts through its factories with barcodes. A standard UPC barcode holds just 12 digits, so boxes needed several barcodes each, and workers had to scan them one by one.

In 1992 an engineer called Masahiro Hara started leading a team to build something better: a code that held much more information and could be read quickly, from any angle.

A board game and a skyscraper

Two ideas shaped the design, according to Hara's own account.

  • The game of Go. In Go, black and white stones sit on the points of a grid, and they're still easy to read when they're slightly out of place. Hara wanted the code's dark and light squares to be just as forgiving.
  • A skyscraper. Looking out of a train window, Hara noticed how a tall building stood out from everything around it. The code needed something like that, a shape that could not be confused with the text and images printed nearby.

That shape became the three large squares in the corners, called finder patterns. The team spent about three months testing patterns against a collection of newspaper images, looking for one that almost never turns up by chance, and settled on bands of dark and light in the ratio 1:1:3:1:1. Because the three squares sit in fixed corners, a scanner can tell which way up the code is from any angle.

Quick Response

Denso released the QR code, short for Quick Response, in 1994. Compared with a barcode, it could hold thousands of characters instead of a dozen digits. The largest version holds up to 7,089 digits.

It was also built to survive the factory floor. Its highest error correction level can rebuild the code even when around 30% of it is dirty or damaged. That same feature is what lets a logo sit in the middle of a QR code today.

Free for everyone to use

Denso held patents on the QR code, but chose not to use them to charge for the standard code, so anyone could make and read QR codes freely. It became an international standard, ISO/IEC 18004, in 2000.

Why the design still works

Every QR code made on this site follows the same standard that grew out of Hara's work. The corner squares still tell a camera where the code is and which way up it sits. The error correction still rebuilds damaged parts. And the clear border around the edge, called the quiet zone, still separates the code from everything else on the page, just like that skyscraper on the skyline.
